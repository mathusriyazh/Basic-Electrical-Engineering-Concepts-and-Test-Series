1. Domestic Wiring

Basic Principles of Domestic Wiring

Domestic wiring is the electrical installation used to supply electrical energy safely to lighting points, fans, sockets, appliances and other loads in a building.

Supply

Electrical energy is supplied from the distribution system through service mains to the consumer installation.

Protection

Fuse, MCB, RCCB and proper earthing are used to protect people and equipment.

Switching

Switches should normally be connected in the phase conductor.

Earthing

Exposed metallic parts of electrical equipment are connected to the protective earthing system.

Types of Domestic Wiring

Figure: Types of Domestic Wiring

Cleat Wiring

  • Wires are supported on cleats.
  • Simple and inexpensive.
  • Easy to install.
  • Suitable mainly for temporary installations.

Wooden Casing-Capping

  • Wires are placed inside casing.
  • Capping provides protection.
  • Neat appearance.
  • Used for indoor wiring.

TRS / CTS Wiring

  • TRS = Tough Rubber Sheathed.
  • CTS = Cab Tyre Sheathed.
  • Good mechanical protection.

Conduit Wiring

  • Conductors are enclosed in conduit.
  • Conduit can be metallic or non-metallic.
  • Excellent mechanical protection.
  • Widely used in modern installations.

Learning Outcome

Students will be able to identify different domestic wiring systems, explain their construction and select an appropriate wiring method for different applications.

2. Domestic Wiring Accessories

Common Accessories

Switch

Controls the supply to an electrical load.

Socket Outlet

Provides connection for portable appliances.

Plug

Connects portable electrical equipment to a socket.

Fuse

Protects the circuit by melting during excessive current.

MCB

Automatically disconnects the circuit during overload or short circuit.

RCCB

Provides protection against earth leakage and electric shock.

Distribution Board

Distributes electrical power to different circuits.

Energy Meter

Measures electrical energy consumption in kWh.

Domestic Wiring Safety

  1. Switch OFF the supply before maintenance.
  2. Use correctly rated protective devices.
  3. Use suitable size conductors.
  4. Provide proper earthing.
  5. Do not overload socket outlets.
  6. Inspect wiring periodically.
Safety: Electrical installation and maintenance should be performed by qualified personnel following applicable electrical safety requirements.

3. Staircase Wiring

Two-Way Switch Staircase Wiring

Staircase wiring allows one lamp to be controlled from two different locations using two two-way switches.

Figure: Staircase Wiring Using Two-Way Switches

Working Principle

  1. Two two-way switches are used.
  2. The first switch is connected to the phase supply.
  3. Two traveller wires connect the switches.
  4. The second switch is connected to the lamp.
  5. The lamp can be switched ON or OFF from either location.

4. Earthing

Definition of Earthing

Earthing is the process of connecting exposed metallic parts of electrical equipment to the general mass of earth through a low-resistance conducting path.

Protection Against Shock

Provides a low-resistance path for fault current.

Fault Current Path

Allows protective devices to operate during faults.

Equipment Protection

Helps protect electrical equipment against insulation faults.

Voltage Reference

Provides a reference potential for the electrical system.

Earthing Resistance ≤ 2 Ω

Types of Earthing

Figure: Types of Earthing

G.I. Pipe Earthing

A galvanized iron pipe is installed vertically in the ground and connected to the electrical installation through an earthing conductor.

Plate Earthing

A copper or galvanized iron plate is buried in the ground and connected to the electrical installation.

Pipe Electrode

The pipe electrode provides a conducting path between the electrical installation and earth.

Maintenance

The earthing system should be inspected periodically and its resistance should be measured.

Animated Fault Current Path

During an insulation fault, fault current flows through the protective earthing path.

Stages of Electrical Supply

Generating Station

Electrical energy is generated using thermal, hydro, nuclear, solar, wind and other energy sources.

Primary Transmission

Generated voltage is stepped up to a high voltage for long-distance transmission.

Receiving Station

The transmitted electrical energy is received and voltage is stepped down.

Distribution Substation

Voltage is reduced to suitable levels for distribution to consumers.

Types of Generation

Thermal

Fuel is used to produce heat and mechanical energy for the generator.

Hydroelectric

Water energy drives hydraulic turbines.

Nuclear

Nuclear energy produces heat for steam generation.

Solar

Photovoltaic cells convert solar energy into electricity.

Wind

Wind turbines convert wind energy into electrical energy.

Feeder, Distributor and Service Mains

Feeder

A feeder carries electrical power from a substation to a distribution area. Normally, consumer tapping is not made along the feeder.

Distributor

A distributor supplies power to consumers through tapping points.

Service Mains

Service mains connect the distributor to the consumer installation.

Why High Voltage Transmission?

P = VI
Ploss = I²R

For a given power, increasing the transmission voltage reduces the current. Because transmission loss varies as the square of current, reducing current reduces conductor losses.

7. Three-Phase Balanced System

Concept

A balanced three-phase system consists of three sinusoidal voltages of equal magnitude and frequency displaced by 120° electrical.

VR = Vm sin(ωt)
VY = Vm sin(ωt − 120°)
VB = Vm sin(ωt − 240°)

Advantages of Three-Phase System

Efficient Transmission

Suitable for transmission of large amounts of power.

Rotating Magnetic Field

Produces a rotating magnetic field in three-phase motors.

Constant Power

Balanced three-phase loads provide nearly constant power transfer.

Economical

Requires less conductor material for a given power transfer.

9. Domestic Energy Consumption

Electrical Energy Calculation

The monthly electrical energy consumption of a domestic appliance can be calculated from its power rating, quantity, operating hours per day and number of operating days per month.

Monthly Energy (kWh) = Power (W) × Quantity × Hours/Day × Days/Month ÷ 1000
